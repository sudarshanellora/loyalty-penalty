Ireland is no longer expensive. Staying put is.

In June 2026 the average rate on a new Irish mortgage was 3.49%, just under the euro area average of 3.51%. It was the first time Ireland dipped below the average since February 2023. A year earlier Irish borrowers paid 0.31 points more than the euro area.

The gap that remains is inside Ireland, between people taking out a loan today and people who took one out earlier and have not moved since. The Central Bank publishes how many home loans sit at each interest rate. The chart shows that spread for each type of lender.

The widest spread is among firms that bought loan books and no longer lend. About 22% of their home loans carry a rate above 5%, against fewer than 1% at banks. Banks still account for most of the households above 4%, because they hold most of the loans.

Far more people move to a new rate with the same lender than move lender: about €586m in June against roughly €175m a month of switching. Those who ask get a good rate. The cost falls on the people who never ask, so the first call is to your own lender.

Why lenders leave it alone

I simulated the loan book of a fictional mid-sized Irish lender: 50,000 home loans worth €7.9bn, with rates drawn from the Central Bank's published spread for banks. No real customer data is used. Then I priced three ways the lender could respond to customers who are paying more than the market.

At today's switching rate, letting customers leave is the cheapest option by a wide margin. Cutting rates for everyone who is 0.5 points or more above the market gives up about a tenth of the book's net interest income. Even a matching offer made only when a solicitor asks for redemption figures roughly breaks even before staff costs. The loyalty penalty lasts because inertia is profitable.

Two things can change that. Switching can keep rising. And since 24 March 2026 the revised Consumer Protection Code requires lenders to show customers how much they could save and to remind them of cheaper options. The table shows what each combination would cost the simulated lender.

Method and limits

  • The Central Bank reports rates in half-point bands. I place every loan at the middle of its band and apply the average balance for its lender type. Placing loans at the bottom or top of each band, or assuming balances a quarter smaller, moves the €316m estimate to between €185m and €385m.
  • €316m is a gap in rates. It is not money every household can collect tomorrow. The published data does not show how many of these loans are inside a fixed term, where leaving early can carry a fee.
  • Some households cannot switch. About 33,400 home loans were in arrears at end-June 2026. Even if every one of them were in the above-4% group, about 146,500 households would remain.
  • The comparison rate is the average new fixed rate. Individual offers depend on the loan-to-value ratio, the energy rating of the home and the lender.
  • The lender results come from a simulated book. The funding cost (2.25%), the response to offers and the link between savings and switching are my assumptions. The switching rate is set to the published market figure.
  • Balances and loan counts for non-bank lenders are from mid-2025, the latest split published. Everything else is 2026 data.
